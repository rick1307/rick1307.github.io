A thesis about the next layer of digital infrastructure

The Global State Machine

AI is getting smarter. Machines are becoming more autonomous. More decisions, payments, permissions, contracts, and transfers will happen without a human touching every step.

That creates a new infrastructure problem: the world needs a shared way to know what is true now, what changed, and who has the right to change it next.
01

AI can think. But thinking is not the same as state.

An AI can decide what should happen next. The world still needs a trusted record of what actually happened.

A machine can negotiate a price, approve a purchase, route a shipment, release a payment, or execute a contract. But after the action, everyone involved needs to agree on the result.

Who owns the asset now?
Was the payment made?
Did this agent have authority to act?
Has the inventory already been sold?
Which version of the record is authoritative?
02

Inside one company, this is easy.

A company can keep its own database. It controls the users, the rules, the permissions, and the final record.

The problem changes when independent parties have to share state.

Now imagine millions of AI agents belonging to different people, banks, companies, governments, machines, and software systems. They will interact constantly. No single private database can automatically be the final authority for all of them.

The more autonomous the world becomes, the more valuable a neutral, shared state machine becomes.

03

Different infrastructure does different jobs.

This is not a story about blockchain replacing everything else. The pieces work together.

AICreates intelligence, decisions, plans, and actions.
Data centersProvide the computation.
NetworksMove information between machines.
Private databasesManage local state inside trusted organizations.
BlockspaceRecords shared, globally consequential state across independent actors.
04

Blockchains are state machines.

A blockchain is not valuable because it has a coin. The coin is not the starting point.

The starting point is the network’s ability to accept a valid state transition, get independent participants to agree on it, and make that new state durable.

Ownership changedA security, token, property right, or digital asset moved from one party to another.
Payment settledValue moved and the settlement record became final.
Permission changedAn agent, person, or machine gained or lost the right to act.
Obligation changedA contract was created, fulfilled, modified, or closed.

Every trusted state transition consumes capacity. That capacity is blockspace.

05

AI makes the state problem bigger.

Humans already create economic state slowly: we sign, approve, transfer, reconcile, settle, and record.

Autonomous software can do those things continuously, at machine speed, across borders and across organizations.

More autonomous machines
↓ More economic interactions
↓ More state changes
↓ More demand for trusted state-transition capacity

That does not mean every computation belongs on-chain. It means the moments that require shared, durable, authoritative state become increasingly important as machine activity expands.

06

Blockspace becomes an economic resource.

Once shared state becomes valuable, the capacity to produce trusted state becomes valuable too.

Different networks will offer different forms of blockspace: cheap, fast, regulated, private, highly secure, specialized, EVM-compatible, non-EVM, institutional, consumer, machine-to-machine.

There does not have to be one winner. Just as the world uses many forms of compute, energy, storage, and bandwidth, it can use many forms of blockspace.

The long-term opportunity is not guessing one perfect chain. It is recognizing that demand for the resource class itself can grow.

07

The coins are exposure to the suppliers.

Native assets matter because they can sit inside the economics of the networks that supply blockspace: paying fees, securing consensus, providing collateral, supporting liquidity, or governing access to scarce network resources.

The investment question is not simply, “Will crypto be adopted?”

It is: which networks will supply valuable state-machine capacity, and how strongly will their native assets capture the economics of that use?

The thesis

The dominant secular wave is not crypto adoption.

Crypto is the label people see. Coins are the instruments people trade. But underneath both is something more fundamental.

The dominant secular wave is growing demand for global state-machine capacity.

We call that capacity blockspace.

AI does not replace the rest of the computing stack. Blockspace does not replace it either. The thesis is that a more autonomous digital economy creates more need for trusted, shared state—and therefore more demand for the infrastructure that provides it.
